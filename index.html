Abstract

In a popular form of augmented reality (AR), the scene is captured with the back-facing camera of a handheld phone or tablet, and annotations are overlaid onto the live video stream. However, the annotations are not integrated into the user’s field of view, and the user is left with the challenging task of translating the annotations from the display to the real world. This challenge can be alleviated by modifying the video frame to approximate what the user would see in the absence of the display, making the display seem transparent.

This paper demonstrates a robust transparent display implementation using only the back-facing camera of a tablet, which was tested extensively over a variety of complex real world scenes. A user study shows that the transparent AR display lets users locate annotations in the real world significantly more accurately than when using a conventional AR display.

How it works

A phone shows the world from its camera, which sits in a corner of the device and has a wider field of view than the angle the screen covers in your eyes. The result is the dual-view problem: what you see on the display does not line up with what you see around it. Re-rendering the frame from your viewpoint would fix this, but it normally needs both head tracking and per-pixel depth, and both fail on real scenes such as foliage, water, or anything the camera cannot see.

We skip both. We assume the user holds the device in a comfortable default position, the way people naturally hold a phone to take a photo, and we map the camera frame to the user’s view with a single homography. Because a homography is a bijective mapping, it never tears holes into the image the way depth-based reprojection does.

Distant geometry assumption

Each display pixel is looked up along the direction of the user’s ray through it. This turns into a simple zoom-in on the camera frame, produces no redundant pixels, and works surprisingly well even for scenes only a few meters away.

Planar proxy approximation

The scene is approximated by a plane, and each pixel is looked up by projecting its intersection with that plane into the camera. This is exact for planar content such as a screen or a poster and is the mode we used in the user study.

Accuracy analysis

We measure transparency error with three quantities: the reprojection error ε (how far a pixel is from where it should be), the share of missing pixels μ, and the share of redundant pixels ρ that the user already sees around the display. In a simulator of a 10.5-inch tablet held 50 cm from the eye, the distant geometry display beats the conventional one at every scene distance beyond 1 m.

Real scenes

Our display keeps the view continuous across the display frame, also when the geometry is close and when objects move. Because it only needs one homography per frame, it runs on low-end devices as well.

User study

Seventeen participants looked through an Android phone at an image on a laptop screen. The phone briefly marked a point with a dot, and participants then clicked where they believed the dot had been. Each person completed 10 trials with a conventional AR display and 10 with our transparent display, in randomized order.

The mean localization error dropped from 23.9 px with the conventional display to 9.2 px with ours, a 61% reduction (t(16) = 12.38, p < 0.0005, Cohen’s d = 3.0). With the conventional display, people have to memorize landmarks and translate them to the real world. With ours, they simply look in the direction the display points to.
